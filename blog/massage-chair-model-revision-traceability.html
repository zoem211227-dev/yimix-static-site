Massage Chair Model and Revision Records: A Buyer Traceability Guide

A model name alone may not describe every controller, electrical configuration, upholstery or packing variation. Maintain a buyer record that connects the ordered configuration to the evidence you approved. Do not invent a factory serial-number system or assume compatibility from appearance.

YIMIX Buyer Resources · Published 20 October 2026

In this guide
  1. Separate the catalogue name from your order reference
  2. Keep a small set of linked records
  3. Check a proposed change before reusing old evidence
  4. Worked example: a controller requested for an earlier delivery
  5. Use the record when handing over the business
  6. Confirm spare parts before ordering

Separate the catalogue name from your order reference

Use the catalogue model to start a discussion, then request a written configuration reference for the proposed order. Record the agreed controller, functions, electrical configuration, finish and packing. If the supplier uses a revision or batch reference, ask what it identifies and where you should record it.

A buyer can maintain an internal record even when the supplier’s document format differs. Make clear which fields come from supplier documents and which are your own identifiers. An internal code should help your team find the evidence; it must not replace or alter the original product identity.

Keep a small set of linked records

Connect the purchase order, agreed specification, sample decision, branding files and packing information. Save the approved version and approval date rather than keeping only the latest attachment in a chat. Keep open questions visible alongside the approved points.

Avoid storing unnecessary customer personal information in a configuration folder. A public product listing may use the model and approved marketing description, while a restricted operational record holds order references and service evidence. Share only the information needed for the particular discussion.

Check a proposed change before reusing old evidence

Ask whether a revised controller, material, accessory or packing arrangement changes the configuration reference. Record what changed and what stayed the same. Do not describe an appearance-only approval as acceptance of a new function or electrical configuration.

For a repeat order, compare the new proposal with the previous accepted record. If the supplier says the configuration is unchanged, retain that confirmation with the new order. If something differs, identify which sample, instructions or quotation needs to be reviewed again.

Worked example: a controller requested for an earlier delivery

A retailer needs help identifying a controller for a chair delivered previously. The outer shell looks like a current catalogue model. Instead of ordering from the photograph alone, the retailer gathers the original order reference, available product identification and a clear controller photograph.

The technical contact should identify the information needed to assess compatibility. Keep any proposed part reference, revision and installation conditions with the response. This guide does not establish that two parts are interchangeable or that stock is available. Specialist work should be assigned to qualified personnel.

Use the record when handing over the business

Show a colleague how to locate the approved specification and the unresolved items without reconstructing months of messages. Keep original files as evidence and a concise index for everyday use. Agree who can approve an updated record and how earlier versions remain identifiable.

A traceability record improves the quality of your questions, but it does not by itself prove certification scope, warranty coverage or production conformity. Those conclusions require their own applicable evidence. Start with a few dependable fields rather than an elaborate spreadsheet nobody maintains.

Confirm massage chair spare parts compatibility before placing an order

When you request a quote for a trial order, ask for a written match between the proposed chair configuration and the spare parts you expect to support. Start with the components your service team is most likely to ask about, such as the handset or its control board. Ask the supplier which identifiers your team can record from the carton, accessible labels or order paperwork without opening the chair.

Carry the decision into the next purchase order

In a hypothetical trial order, the supplier confirms handset A for the quoted chair. A repeat quotation lists the same catalogue model with a different controller configuration. Keep the original handset confirmation attached to the trial order. For the repeat order, ask whether handset A is approved for the new configuration or whether the service team needs a second part reference. Mark the match as pending until the supplier answers in writing.

Choose the proposed chair from the product catalogue and include its model, destination market and planned quantities in the inquiry below. For destination-specific variants, use the electrical configuration checklist to identify the documents you need alongside the parts confirmation.

Build a parts-identification packet for an earlier order

A retailer may receive a service question after the original purchasing contact has changed roles. Start with an identification packet that connects the affected chair to the original order. A current catalogue photograph can help explain the question, but it cannot establish that a component fits an earlier configuration. This record is a buyer's administrative tool, not a repair procedure.

Separate the observation from the diagnosis

Record what was reported and when it was first noticed. “The controller does not respond” is an observation; “the controller must be replaced” is a diagnosis requiring technical assessment. Include original model and order references, available product labels and safe external photographs. If an identifier is missing or unreadable, say so. Do not create a serial number or guess the production revision.

Do not open covers, handle electrical components or reproduce a fault to obtain evidence. Ask the technical contact what information can be collected safely. Remove customer names, addresses and personal conversations before sharing the identification material. A supplier needs the relevant operational references, not an unrestricted customer file.

Example: two similar deliveries, one unresolved identity

Imagine a retailer has received two orders of chairs with similar shells. A customer reports a control problem. Before requesting a component from a current product image, the retailer locates the affected chair's order record. If the available labels do not distinguish the deliveries, the request remains an identification question. A photograph of a similar controller is a reference, not approval to substitute it.

This hypothetical example does not claim that YIMIX changed a controller between deliveries. It shows why the buyer should keep original identifiers. Ask the technical contact to connect the proposed component to the evidence and identify anything still unresolved. A parts quotation does not by itself confirm warranty coverage, installation suitability or stock availability.

Close the administrative step without closing the service case

Keep the technical reply with the original request. Use distinct statuses such as identity confirmed, more information required, quotation requested and referred for qualified assessment. Identifying or dispatching a component does not prove that the customer's issue is resolved. Charges, freight, support responsibilities and warranty applicability require written confirmation for the specific case.

For a new purchase, use the sourcing brief to ask which identification information accompanies the order. Review the support questions before making retail promises. Use the existing inquiry below with the model and available order reference; omit customer personal information.
